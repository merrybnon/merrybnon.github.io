---
title: "On Stillness or No Motion?"
categories: [philosophy]
tags: [philosophy]
---

The impetus for this little piece of writing was a memory of a girl I met, or more was in the same room as, back in January 2025. At the time, I was wwoofing for a guy named Ken up near Santa Cruz. Twice a week (if I remember correctly) we would go up to a communeish religious retreat spot called the Mount Madonna Center for yoga classes. The classes always had this one girl, also early twenties, who was just so impressively still. Before the class started, she was often in a child’s pose esque position, completely motionless, only breaking the pose when the teacher began speaking. Not even a small change of position. Throughout the class, she would hold the poses again with impressive stillness, which, while often the goal, is not commonly accomplished by a yoga participator. I wish that I had asked her how she did it, especially at such a young age, but with no answer from the horse’s mouth, I can speculate my own.

(This is where I take it off the rails btw). What is impressive about stillness? It is impressive because it conveys total contentedness, total security, and total lack of desire. Motion is change. Motion is a change of state. I mean this very much in a physics sense. Motion is quite literally the change in position, and in a generalized momentum sense is a change of some state variable at large. When desire is involved, the desire for motion is implicitly the desire to be in a better state than you are currently in. Stillness, the rejection of motion, is thus the rejection of change, and a rejection of the premise that there is a better state to be in. This is why I say stillness is contentedness, security, and lack of desire. 

You can feel this explicitly by doing a simple exercise: try to remain completely still. I like to do this in the sauna, or that’s where I’ve gotten into the habit of it anyway. I’ll sit with a straight back, eyes closed, hands loosely clasped, and try to have no motion of my body. You very quickly become aware of what drives you to motion. Mostly, it is subconscious urges, things you would not normally have awareness of. An itch develops on your nose, the door opens and closes, a weird feeling in your leg. The practice of stillness is the practice of awareness of one of your most basic urges: movement. It is a constant rejection of the urge to react, of the urge to respond, of the urge to change. The ultimate rejection, though, is to reject without rejecting, to do without thinking, wu wei. True stillness comes not from a constant effort of the rejection of movement, but a total absence of the urge to move in the first place. True stillness is an unconscious process, without awareness. This is what the girl in Santa Cruz achieved. The urge to move, the desire for change, was completely absent from her, she rejected without rejecting, she did without thinking. Study this deeply.
