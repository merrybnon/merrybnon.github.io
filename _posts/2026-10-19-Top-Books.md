I had just sat down to do a little bit of YouTube with my dinner, watching Man Carrying Thing rank his top ten books of all time. I thought to myself, I’ve read more than ten books, surely I have a top ten books of all time. Reading through my catalogue of books I’ve read since the start of 2023, I realized that, especially if I’m limiting myself to only one per author, I wouldn’t make a top ten list. In fact, there are only four (maybe five) books that I’m truly jumping around the wall, bouncing up and down, kicking my little feet and giggling, excited about, plus honorable mentions.

## Honorable Mentions
 
### Ursula K. Le Guin
 
Making this list, I confronted something I’ve always known which is I really mostly read books by men. So I was tempted to throw one of her books, all of which I’ve really enjoyed, onto the list. But, that would be performative, and while I really enjoyed both *Left Hand of Darkness* and *A Wizard of Earthsea*, I wouldn’t consider either of them as top of the list books. Maybe one day I’ll read *The Dispossessed* and finally become a feminist by putting it at the top. (I’ve actually read it since originally writing this list, it was very good, but didn’t make the top four).
 
### Apology
 
A common theme among my favorites and my reading in general is philosophy. This is certainly my favorite piece of ancient philosophy and has remained that way through the other of Plato’s dialogues I’ve read (which were mainly kinda mid). Socrates spits wisdom on topics ranging from how to judge yourself, to morality, to epistemic humility, tons of gems in here. The only reason it doesn’t make the top is that it’s not a book :/, and the five dialogues together definitely are not making this list.
 
### Dao De Jing
 
Why not keep the philosophy ball rolling. I own too many copies of this book, I believe four, including the Ursula K. Le Guin version, which I found quite enjoyable. Lots of great wisdom in here, especially on the nature of reality. “The Dao that can be named is not the Dao”. Such a simple line, but it really elegantly encapsulates a fundamental part of our attempts to describe Nature. Whatever we come up with is always an abstraction, it is never the real thing, the fundamental, the Dao, that just is. Maybe after my next read I’ll put it in the top, but it always feels like there’s too much random shit in there to really make it.
 
### Introduction to the Theory of Complex Systems
 
This is both its own pick and a stand-in for many similar reads. Essentially, I read a number of technical, fairly dry, sometimes boring, extremely informative books, that will completely reshape the way I view the world or aspects of it. (*Fractals: Form Chance Dimension, Energy and Civilization, On the Origin of Species,* etc.). They are a double edged sword, in that they greatly inform my worldview, bring me huge knowledge, and make me a better person, but I will almost inevitably put them down for like a whole month before, through will alone, making myself finish them. This did make me reflect that those first points are really much more what I value from a book. For example, I didn’t even consider putting something like *Babel* on here, and that thing had me gripped the whole time. But it was nothing more than the grip, I mean the whole time I was like this is all so obvious, I mean the message was essentially colonialism is bad with the only edge being violence is ok to overthrow colonial systems, hahaha. Again, a fun read and a message I agree with, but I didn’t consider it for my list. The books I did put had both the grip and the value.
 
### Dune Series
 
Banger series that I read over the course of like four years. I really enjoyed it, and it had both the value and the grip I was talking about earlier, but I couldn’t really choose just one, and if I could I don’t know that I would put it in the top. If I did choose, it’d probably be something from 2-4. They all are slowly upping the slow-burn fascinating conversation scene from the first, I don’t know 1, 5, and 6 were great too, haha, who’s to say.
 
### Hitchhiker’s Guide To The Galaxy
 
This one’s close. It was so much fun to read, which was also influenced by the fact that I was high (and in the woods) for the two nights that I read this over, it’s maybe the best book you could read while high. This could also easily be the funniest book I’ve ever read, and while the later entries in the series felt absurd in a more random way, this was absurd in a way that made complete coherent sense at the same time. If I give it a second read and it still blows me away, who knows, maybe it’ll make the list.
 
### The Stranger
 
If this was a top five this book would be five. It so perfectly captures the meaningless life, and as someone who thinks life is meaningless, it was, in that way, relatable. Just the way he floats through life, it captures a certain feeling that I think we all can experience at some times. Not to mention that ending, holy moly. His monologue hit me like a truck, no convert here. Also, I’ve had *The Fall,* also by Camus, praised to me a couple times, but I did not fuck with it as much, and I’d recommend this one much more to anyone deciding between the two. What’s holding it back from the top? Honestly it’s probably just a tax for the fact I’ve only read it one time, but I definitely plan to get the second reading in soon, so stay tuned. (Future Hudson here again, I also read *The Myth of Sisyphus* over this summer. I really enjoyed it, too, I’d place it between *The Stranger* and *The Fall* in a ranking).
 
## The Top Four
 
### Antifragile
 
Yeah, I like a little math, I like a little philosophy, I like a little systems theory. If you know me, you may have had the unfortunate experience of hearing me talk about Nassim Taleb. Unfortunate because this guy is an asshole (self-proclaimed), hahaha, and comes across that way in his writing too. If anything you have to give him some credit of maybe anti-fakeness in that I hear he’s actually quite enjoyable and funny in real life. The guy aside, this has been an extremely impactful book on my life and worldview. The book is essentially the blueprint of how to deal with (and benefit from) uncertainty. And life, time, the future, are all uncertain. I originally read about a half to two-thirds of it back in high school as part of a philosophy independent study I was part of, then read the whole thing in the summer between Junior and Senior year. This book distills all the best parts of Taleb’s philosophy, and will forever be part of the way I view life.
 
### Siddhartha
 
The only book of my top four that I’ve only read once, it was just that impactful, even on the first read. The book follows the life of one man (Siddhartha), specifically the spiritual journey he goes on to eventually find enlightenment. Is it crazy that it I’m like I gotta model my life off of this? Dare I say, the final message is Daoist, or that’s my interpretation anyway. Finding enlightenment in the river? Very Daoist, but it also feels very earned, coming at the end of a long process with many rocks turned. (I reread this over the summer as well, I was actually not as high on it the second time around. The first time hit me hard, and the second time was just quite interesting. I’ll still keep it on here for the sake of leaving the list as it was, but I’d maybe even have *The Stranger* above it these days, hard to say).
 
### Blindsight
 
What a strong book, it’s not holding any punches. It tackles the big subjects, consciousness, evolution, human-technology arms races, and comes away with a dark but compelling vision of what the future holds. Watts’s version of the later 21<sup>st</sup> century is extremely inhuman, and the people are forced to become more inhuman themselves in order to keep up. That’s just the backdrop, though, with the main thrust coming from the extremely alien life form that has appeared on humanity’s map. The book has a biologist’s fingerprints all over it, it takes evolution very seriously. The writing hits hard, too. I’m thinking specifically of Siri’s struggle to say goodbye, and the two moments of violence in the ship. I still think about ideas from this book, and no doubt will for the rest of my life. I wasn’t as impressed with the sequel, it felt like if this book hadn’t stuck the landing, but I am psyched for the third book, and will no doubt be reading it immediately upon release.
 
### Slaughterhouse Five
 
One of my most read books of all time (although that’s only three or four, haha). Wow, wow, wow. I originally read it in tenth grade, and immediately fell in love with Kurt Vonnegut’s writing. In fact, he has been my favorite author since my original reading of this book. I’m on a (slow) mission to one day have read all his books, and the main reason I’m limiting myself to one book per author is I’d probably put at least *Cat’s Cradle* also on this list, and maybe *Sirens of Titan* too. All three so perfectly capture the kind of humanist postmodernism that makes his writing so strong. On one hand, he can so easily point to the meaninglessness of life, the absurdity of it all, yet shining through is always the beauty of the human experience too. This book is funny too, and perfectly satirizes everything from war, to mid century American life, to time itself. I forget where I read this (it may even be in the book), but Vonnegut said that there are no characters in the book. I disagree, in fact I think one of the *strongest* aspects is the characters, they’re so vivid, so absurd, so human, and I love them for that.
